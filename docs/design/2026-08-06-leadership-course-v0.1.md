## The spine (binding - from the research)

- Aim is a **capacity, not a title**: to *serve a group well, and be worth following.* "Leader" never means
  "in charge" or "the one people obey." Greenleaf's servant-leader is **servant first** - the impulse to
  serve is native and comes *before* the choice to lead. Beneficence is the origin, not a bolt-on.
- **The honest test is the firewall** (Greenleaf's "best test"): does what you do leave the people you
  served *more capable and free* - and does it help the ones with the least? The tell that runs through
  every step: **are you becoming worth following, or just trying to make others follow?**
- **Experiential, not lectured** (the developmental evidence): youth leadership is a measurable, buildable
  capacity (YLPS); the service-orientation under it actually *declines* through adolescence unless the
  environment feeds it (Wray-Lake 2016); and it is a young leader's *prosociality*, not their status, that
  makes their presence lift a group instead of harm it (Child Development 2026). So every step is a doing.
- **Specimen firewall.** Greene's *48 Laws of Power* and Machiavelli's *Prince* are studied - if at all -
  only as specimens of leadership-as-domination (what it sells, how to recognize it), never as advice. The
  course never teaches "how to get people to follow you."

## The arc (Serve -> Steady -> Gather - validated by the research, not invented)

Leadership and domination start at the same door: a group, and someone out front. The whole ethics is what
you do when you get there. **Serve** (go first, fill a real need) -> **Steady** (become someone a group can
rely on) -> **Gather** (the group chooses the one who has served). The one test runs through all three:
*worth following, or making others follow?*

---

## Stage 1 - SERVE  ·  *"The servant-leader is servant first."* (Greenleaf, 1970)

Leadership begins as service, before it is ever a role. Going first, helping without being asked. No
title, no permission, nothing you don't already have.

1. **Go first.** Do one small thing that helps a group before anyone asks - pick up what got dropped,
   start the dull job, welcome the new person. The act of going first, once, is the whole lesson.
2. **Serve the real need.** Find the thing your group (family, team, class, friends) *actually* needs done -
   not the thing that makes you look good, the thing that's genuinely needed - and do it. (A need that
   *exists*, not one you invented.)
3. **Serve without the credit.** Do one helpful thing where no one will know it was you. Notice whether you
   can. (This is the tell: service centers the group; performance centers you.)
4. **Name who you'd serve.** What group or cause would you carry something hard for, even when it costs you?
   (A direction, not a title.)

## Stage 2 - STEADY  ·  Be someone a group can rely on.

You become worth following one kept word at a time. This stage is self-leadership - steadiness, responsibility,
owning your misses - and it holds the firewall step.

1. **Keep one word.** Promise your group one small thing and do it - exactly, on time. Being trusted is
   built, not claimed.
2. **Stay steady when it's hard.** Next time something goes wrong in a group, be the one who stays calm and
   asks *"what do we do now?"* instead of blaming. (Steadiness under pressure is the core of self-leadership.)
3. **Own one miss.** When you get something wrong or drop it, say so plainly and fix it - no excuse, no
   hiding. A group trusts the person who owns their miss faster than the person who never admits one.
4. **Lift, don't push (the honest test).** Catch yourself once: are you helping the group get somewhere
   *good*, or getting people to do what *you* want? That's the whole fork - *worth following vs make others
   follow.* When you catch the second, stop.

## Stage 3 - GATHER  ·  The group chooses the one who has served.

Real authority isn't taken; it's granted - "freely and knowingly, in proportion to the clearly evident
servant stature of the leader" (Greenleaf). And the aim isn't more followers - it's more people able to
lead themselves.

1. **Make room for another voice.** In a group you're part of, draw out someone quieter - ask what they
   think, and mean it. (Servant-leaders make more servant-leaders, not more followers.)
2. **Organize one real thing.** Pull a few people together to do something that helps beyond yourselves - a
   clean-up, a project, helping a neighbor - and carry the responsibility for it through to done.
3. **Ask the best test.** Afterward, ask Greenleaf's question about yourself: did the people I served grow -
   more capable, more free - or did I just get my way? And did it help the ones with the least? Answer
   honestly.
4. **Hand it on.** Give away a piece of what you lead - let someone else carry it, and teach them how.
   Leadership you can't hand on wasn't service; it was just power you were holding.
