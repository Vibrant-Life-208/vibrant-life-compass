## The spine (binding - from the research)

- Aim is a **capacity, not a title or a payout**: to *make something that serves a real need.*
  "Entrepreneur" never means "be your own boss," "get rich," or "build a startup." It means: find a
  need that already exists, build something real that fills it, and leave people better for it.
  Beneficence is native - a business is a way to serve, not a way to extract.
- **Wardlaw's one word is the whole start: EXIST.** "Find a need and fill it" - but the need must
  *already exist.* The honest venture serves a need that is already there; the extractive one
  manufactures the worry and sells relief from it. That is Wardlaw vs Karbo, at company scale.
- **The firewall (the honest test), extended to ventures:** does the venture leave people with a
  real good they're better for - a capacity, a solved need - or with an addiction / anxiety /
  captured attention? The tell: is the engine *"fill a need that exists"* or *"capture attention /
  manufacture want / lock people in"?* A venture whose engine is manufactured need or captured
  attention is Karbo at company scale. This is evoke-not-extract, applied to teaching kids to build.
- **Experiential, evidence over founder-myth.** Making is build -> ship -> iterate: the smallest
  real thing, put in front of one real person, improved from what actually happened. Founder-lore
  ("I had the vision") is survivorship bias - you hear the winners and never the wreckage. Every
  step is a doing.

## The arc (See -> Make -> Serve - validated by the research, not invented)

Every venture and every con start at the same door: a need, and someone who could fill it. The whole
ethics is what you do there. **See** (find a need that already exists - Wardlaw's word) -> **Make**
(build the smallest real thing, ship it, improve it; and refuse the extraction shortcut) -> **Serve**
(does it leave people genuinely better? serve, don't hustle). The one test runs through all three:
*does it fill a real need, or manufacture / capture one?*

---

## Stage 1 - SEE  ·  *"Filling needs that exist."* (Wardlaw, 1958)

Every honest venture starts at a need that is already there - not one you talk someone into. Needs no
capital, no idea, no permission: just notice, and check that the need is real.

1. **Spot a real need.** Notice one thing someone around you actually needs done or made - real,
   already there, not something you'd have to convince them to want. (Wardlaw's whole title: *find a
   need that EXISTS and fill it.*)
2. **Ask the person, don't guess.** Talk to someone who has the need and ask what would actually
   help. The need is theirs to describe, not yours to assume. Listen before you build.
3. **Check it's real, not manufactured (the first firewall check).** Ask: does this need already
   exist - or would I have to *create the worry* to sell the fix? If you'd have to manufacture it,
   drop it. (Manufacturing the need is Karbo, at company scale.)
4. **Pick the one you'd be glad to fill.** Of the real needs you see, choose the one you'd be proud
   to spend real effort on. A direction, not a business plan.

## Stage 2 - MAKE  ·  Build the smallest real thing, ship it, improve it.

Making is not a vision - it's a loop: build -> ship -> learn -> build again. The smallest real
version beats the perfect imagined one. This stage holds the firewall.

1. **Make the smallest real thing.** Build the tiniest version that actually works - not the dream
   version, the one a real person could use this week. (Shipping something real beats planning
   something perfect.)
2. **Put it in front of one real person.** Give it to one actual person with the need and watch what
   happens. Real use, not your imagination, tells you the truth about what you made. If they don't want
   it, that's data about the thing, never a verdict on you. *(SSC pre-review fix E-1, 2026-08-10:
   failure-as-data shock-absorber sits beside the exposure step - a thing people don't want is
   information, not a judgment on the child.)*
3. **Improve from what happened, not what you hoped.** Change one thing based on what the real person
   actually did or said - not what you wish they'd wanted. That's the whole loop: make, ship, learn,
   make again.
4. **Refuse the extraction shortcut (the honest test).** Notice one way you *could* make it
   "stickier" - a hook, an anxiety, something that makes it hard to leave - and choose not to. The
   test: does it leave people better off, or just harder to escape? And never make *yourself* the
   product: build something that serves a need - don't sell yourself or farm an audience. *Fill a
   need, never capture attention.* (Evoke, never extract - at company scale.) *(SSC pre-review fix E-2,
   2026-08-10: explicit "you are not the product" beat against the kidfluencer / self-as-product pull -
   HARMFUL for minors if it ever creeps in.)*

## Stage 3 - SERVE  ·  Does it leave people genuinely better? Serve, don't hustle.

A venture is a way to serve. The aim isn't to get rich or "crush it" - it's to leave the people you
built for genuinely better off, and to keep the making honest.

1. **Ask the capacity test.** Of the thing you made, ask: does the person walk away with something
   they're genuinely better for - a real good, a solved need - or only a feeling I sold them?
   Capacity, not a feeling. (The same test the money course keeps around Hill and Karbo.)
2. **Trade fair, both sides ahead.** If money changes hands, ask whether *both* sides come out ahead.
   A fair trade leaves both richer; extraction leaves you richer and them emptier. Price like you'd
   want to be priced to.
3. **Give some of it back.** Set aside a part of what you make - time, help, or money - for someone
   or something beyond you. A business is a way to serve; beneficence is native, not a bolt-on.
4. **Refuse the hustle story.** Find one "crush it / grind / scale at all costs / be your own boss"
   message and name what it's selling. Real building is slow, serves a need that exists, and doesn't
   require burning yourself or your customers. (Survivorship bias: you hear the founders who won,
   never the wreckage behind them.)
